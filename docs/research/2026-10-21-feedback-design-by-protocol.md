# Keep the signal fast, make the reward forgiving

The field's best evidence says two things that pull in different directions, and our own measurements have just made the clash visible. For burst-style rhythms (SMR and alpha), the feedback has to reach the trainee **while the burst is still happening**. The one experiment that varied delay found that lasting alpha change happened at about a quarter of a second and **did not happen at three-quarters of a second** ([Belinskaya et al. 2020](https://publications.hse.ru/mirror/pubs/share/direct/424296808.pdf)). The new, faster filter (cFIR, in the `band_envelope` stage) moves us toward that fast end. But a faster, truer signal flickers more, and our reward rules ask for 250 ms or 1 s of **unbroken** success on **two or three conditions at once**. So time spent earning a reward fell to 0.4–10 % in every protocol, far below the 25–40 % used in positive clinical work ([Arns et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4274876/)). The answer is not to go back to the slow filter. It is to leave the signal fast and loosen the reward rule: an always-on tone that follows the trained band, a separate "ding" when the band stays up for a while, a mute when muscle noise appears, fewer conditions joined by AND, and a rule that tolerates brief dips. That design has no head-to-head trial behind it, but it fits every camp in the literature. Three things are worth knowing up front. Filter speed barely matters for the slow protocols (alpha-theta, coherence, asymmetry, HRV). The choice between cFIR and the IIR alternative is a minor decision. And for most of the library, the evidence for clinical benefit is weaker than the evidence that people can learn to shift the signal.

**How to read the evidence labels.** **[Trial]** means a controlled study (sham, mock feedback, or a comparison group) or a meta-analysis of such studies. **[Small/uncontrolled]** means a pilot, a single group with no control, a case series, or a controlled study with roughly 10 people per arm. **[Expert theory]** means a learning-theory argument or a consensus paper with no direct test. **[Convention]** means clinical habit, vendor manuals or practitioner writing. **[Ours]** means our own bench measurements: 13 resting EEG recordings replayed through the pipelines. These are not training trials, and nobody learned anything in them.

## Our filter change exposed a reward rule that only worked on a blurred signal

**What we measured [Ours].** The old envelope was a four-step chain: a Butterworth band-pass filter, a Hilbert step that turns the wave into its outline, a magnitude step, and then 250 or 500 ms of smoothing. Replacing it with cFIR (`band_envelope`, a single filter designed to recover the band's outline quickly) made every envelope both **faster and more accurate**. On SMR, for example, the delay fell from 568 to 360 ms and the correlation with a reference outline rose from 0.889 to 0.915. Over the same recordings, the share of time spent earning a reward collapsed:

| Protocol | Old pipeline | cFIR |
|---|---|---|
| `smr_classic_baseline_staged_cz` | 27.5 % | 8.9 % |
| `placement_smr_bipolar` | 21.1 % | 10.1 % |
| `alpha_theta_narrowband` | 16.2 % | 3.6 % |
| `smr` | 14.7 % | 3.4 % |
| `beta_focus_staged_fz` | 12.4 % | 1.0 % |
| `beta_attention` | 12.3 % | 3.3 % |
| `high_beta_down` | 10.3 % | 0.4 % |
| `alpha_up` | 8.5 % | 1.7 % |
| `alpha_theta` | 39.9 % | 0.7 % |

The cause is that the old smoothing made the signal look **steadier than the brain really is**. On 12–15 Hz, the old outline crossed its threshold about 1.3 times a second, while the true outline crosses about 2.0 times a second and the accurate filters show 2.3–2.6. A reward rule that requires 250 ms (or, in alpha-theta, 1 s) above threshold **with no break** fails whenever the signal flickers. With two or three conditions joined by AND, a flicker on any one of them resets the clock.

**What the literature says this means.** SMR naturally comes in short spindles. The expert recommendation is to reward a spindle of about 0.25 s rather than any brief poke above threshold ([Sherlin et al. 2011](https://isnr-jnt.org/article/view/16552)) **[Expert theory]**. The old pipeline's smoothing was quietly doing that "sustained" filtering, at a cost of roughly 250 ms of extra delay. Commercial systems put that job into the reward logic instead of the signal. BrainMaster exposes a "sustained reward" time (default 500 ms) and a "refractory period", which is a lockout after each reward ([BrainMaster 3.0 manual](https://brainmaster.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/531-320_BrainMaster3.0UserManual_v2.2_3-16-16.pdf)) **[Convention]**. A thresholding patent requires the target to hold for "preferably 100 to 600 milliseconds" ([US 11116440](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11116440)) **[Convention]**. None of these hold times has been tested in a controlled study.

Our collapsed numbers are also far below anything a positive study used. Arns 2014 set thresholds for **25–40 % effective reinforcement** ([Arns et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4274876/)) **[Small/uncontrolled]**. The large blinded ADHD trial set theta below threshold about 60 % of the time and beta above it about 40 %, and held those thresholds fixed for five sessions "allowing the child to experience considerable success" ([NCG protocol](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/43/NCT02251743/Prot_SAP_000.pdf)) **[Trial, protocol document]**. The only experiment that compared reward rates directly ran one session with 56 people. Only the group rewarded **about 80 % of the time** raised SMR, and the authors pointed out that requiring three bars at once pushes the real reward rate well below each bar's nominal rate ([Nam & Choi 2020](https://neuroregulation.org/article/view/20383)) **[Small/uncontrolled]**. A trainee who earns a reward 1–3 % of the time is barely being rewarded at all.

**So the fix has three parts.** Keep the fast filter. Move "sustained" into a forgiving reward rule. One way is a two-line rule: the reward switches on when the band rises above an upper line and switches off only when it drops below a lower line, so a burst's natural flicker doesn't end it. The other is a hold timer that tolerates dips of, say, 50–100 ms. Then measure the resulting reward rate on real sessions and tune thresholds to hit it. The exact tolerance values are a design choice that no study has settled.

## SMR family: the band is settled, the reward rule is not

*Applies to `smr.refrain`, `placement_smr_bipolar.refrain`, `placement_smr_set.refrain` and `smr_classic_baseline_staged_cz.refrain`.*

**Band and site.** SMR (12–15 Hz over the sensorimotor strip, C3/Cz/C4) is one of three "standard protocols" for ADHD, alongside theta/beta and slow cortical potentials ([Enriquez-Geppert et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6538574/)) **[Trial, review]**. In one open clinical series, SMR and theta/beta reduced symptoms equally. SMR also halved the time taken to fall asleep (40 to 19 min), and that sleep change tracked the attention gains, which suggests SMR works partly through better sleep ([Arns et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4274876/)) **[Small/uncontrolled]**. Across trials, the type of protocol did not predict outcome, while EEG quality and eye-artifact correction did ([Bussalb et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2019.00035/full)) **[Trial, meta-regression]**. The sobering part is that the best-blinded trial (theta/beta, 144 children, a sham that replayed recorded EEG) found **no advantage over sham** ([NCG 2021](https://www.em-consulte.com/article/1454575/double-blind-placebo-controlled-randomized-clinica)), and a 2025 meta-analysis limited to blinded ratings found no meaningful group-level benefit ([JAMA Psychiatry 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39661381/)) **[Trial]**. A meta-analysis of healthy adults found SMR the most trainable band during sessions (g = 0.66, against 0.18 for alpha) ([Galang et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12426165/)) **[Trial, meta-analysis]**. Our band and site choices are therefore defensible. They do not guarantee a clinical effect.

**Feedback type.** Sterman's original 1970s setup was discrete: when every criterion held for 0.5 s, a green light came on and a "ding" sounded ([Sherlin et al. 2011](https://isnr-jnt.org/article/view/16552)). Arns 2014 used a hold of 0.15–0.5 s with bar graphs plus sound. The case that discrete reward beats continuous feedback is theory, and its authors conceded that no study had tested it. Their main mechanism, a brief brain "settling" after each reward that they called post-reinforcement synchronization, has since been described by the same group as **"not been objectively observed"** ([Kerson, Sherlin & Davelaar 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13525051/)) **[Expert theory]**. A Gruzelier-line review recommends continuous information *plus* a separate reward ([Strehl 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4222234)) **[Expert theory]**. Your proposed design (always-on SMR tone, ding for sustained SMR, mute on muscle) is the hybrid both camps point toward. It is untested head-to-head.

**Inhibits.** Arns 2014 used a theta inhibit in only **8 of 27** SMR patients. Everyone had a muscle inhibit (55–100 Hz kept under 5–10 µV), and no high-beta AND condition was used ([Arns et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4274876/)). Our three-way AND (SMR up, theta down, high-beta down) asks for more than the positive work did. With only one gated tone, the trainee cannot tell which of the three conditions cost them the reward, a "credit assignment" problem in reinforcement-learning terms ([Lubianiker et al. 2022](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/neurofeedback-through-the-lens-of-reinforcement-learning/)) **[Expert theory]**. Treat muscle as a mute rather than as a reward condition, and make theta optional.

**Thresholds.** The four files set thresholds three different ways. `smr.refrain` and `placement_smr_bipolar.refrain` use percentile thresholds recomputed over a rolling 2-minute window, which is a form of auto-thresholding. `placement_smr_set.refrain` uses a fixed 8 µV threshold that ignores the trainee's own level. `smr_classic_baseline_staged_cz.refrain` seeds its thresholds once from a baseline. The critique is that a moving threshold rewards a fixed percentage of the time even while the trainee drifts the wrong way. The blinded trial's experts rejected a system that re-tuned every few seconds and instead held thresholds fixed for five sessions ([NCG protocol](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/43/NCT02251743/Prot_SAP_000.pdf); [Sherlin et al. 2011](https://isnr-jnt.org/article/view/16552)) **[Expert theory]**. Its own authors admit that no study has compared auto with manual thresholds. `smr_classic_baseline_staged_cz` already sets its thresholds from a baseline and holds them, which is closer to the trial practice. For the others, the cheap improvement is to set thresholds from the warm-up and hold them for each 5-minute block, adjusting only between blocks.

**Session structure.** Positive and trial protocols used about 30–40 sessions at 2–3 a week, each with 20–30 min of training split into 5-min blocks with short rests ([Arns et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4274876/)). More sessions packed into fewer weeks went with larger effects ([Bussalb et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2019.00035/full)) **[Trial, meta-regression]**. The staged 4 × 5-min layout matches this. The 20-min continuous `training` phase in `placement_smr_bipolar` and `placement_smr_set` is acceptable but would benefit from rest breaks.

**The placement variants.** Choosing the SMR site per person (C3, Cz or C4, located by thumb-motor stimulation) is what Arns did, but it was never tested against a fixed Cz **[Convention]**. That supports `placement_smr_set` as an option rather than a requirement. For `placement_smr_bipolar`, measuring between two scalp sites cancels much of the shared noise. Studies with more than one active electrode did slightly better ([Bussalb et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2019.00035/full)), and a local-difference recording (a surface Laplacian) largely avoids muscle noise at central sites ([Pope et al. 2022](https://doaj.org/article/e8d598e19f8f4589ad004efb303a0fa6)). A bipolar pair is not a full Laplacian, and practitioners argue it partly trains the *difference* between sites rather than SMR itself ([J Neurotherapy](https://isnr-jnt.org/article/view/16673/10639)) **[Convention]**. Its comparatively high remaining reward time (10.1 % [Ours]) probably reflects that cleaner, less flickery signal.

## Beta protocols: the main risk is rewarding muscle tension

*Applies to `beta_attention.refrain` (15–18 Hz) and `beta_focus_staged_fz.refrain` (13–19 Hz at Fz).*

In healthy volunteers, 15–18 Hz ("beta1") training sped up reaction times and enlarged an attention-related brain response. SMR training instead reduced lapses, which suggests beta1 acts on arousal rather than attention in general ([Egner & Gruzelier 2004](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/770354)) **[Trial, small]**. No clinical head-to-head trial of SMR against 15–18 Hz exists. The 13–19 Hz band at Fz has no trial of its own. It sits inside the 12–21 Hz "beta" used in theta/beta work at frontal and central sites ([Enriquez-Geppert et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6538574/)) **[Convention]**.

The decisive finding is a warning. In a sham-controlled study of beta1 up-training (15–22 Hz), **7 of 14 trainees learned to raise muscle tension instead of beta**, and once they were removed the rest showed no beta change at all. The system had no automatic muscle gating and updated every 200–500 ms. Watching by the trainer did not catch it ([Paluch et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5357644/)) **[Trial, small]**. Muscle noise from even a light (15 %) forehead or temple contraction reaches above 12 Hz at the top of the head. Forehead muscle peaks at **20–30 Hz** ([Goncharova et al. 2003](https://www.neurotechcenter.org/sites/default/files/misc/EMG%20contamination%20of%20EEG%20spectral%20and%20topographical%20characteristics.pdf)), and the Fz site sits right under it. Paluch's recommendations, in order: automatic online muscle detection should be standard; a high-band amplitude limit that withholds reward and interrupts training, calibrated per system, because muscle drove feedback even at 70 µV or less; dedicated face or neck muscle sensors if possible; and the reminder that offline cleanup cannot undo rewards already given.

What both beta files need, then, is everything listed for SMR (always-on tone, forgiving ding, fewer ANDs, held thresholds) plus a **muscle mute that also silences the continuous tone**, triggered from a band above the trained range and set per person. Without that, an always-on tone tracking beta would reward clenching continuously. Their collapse to 3.3 % and 1.0 % reward time [Ours] means the high-beta AND is failing constantly. At Fz much of that "high beta" is probably forehead muscle, which is a further reason to move it from reward condition to mute. `beta_focus_staged_fz` also needs blink handling, because blinks dominate frontal recordings ([Mennella et al. 2017](https://www.research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3307297/2/Mennella%20et%20al.pdf)). Logging each session's high-frequency power would let you spot a trainee who has become a "muscle learner".

## High-beta down-training mostly teaches the jaw to relax

*Applies to `high_beta_down.refrain` (22–30 Hz).*

The evidence is thin. The largest study (87 patients with depression and anxiety, 10 sessions at P3/P4) found that high-beta down-training reduced symptoms, **but no more than frontal asymmetry training**, and it had no sham arm ([Wang et al. 2019](https://pure.lib.cgu.edu.tw/en/publications/the-effects-of-alpha-asymmetry-and-high-beta-down-training-neurof/)) **[Small/uncontrolled]**. One registered alpha-theta trial used high-beta down-training *as its sham* ([NCT02964520](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02964520)), which tells you how inert some investigators think it is. Elsewhere, high beta appears mostly as an inhibit inside other protocols, for example a 20–30 Hz inhibit alongside SMR for insomnia, in groups of 8–12 people ([PMC8546766](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8546766/)).

The physiology matters more than the trials here. In people temporarily paralysed for an experiment, scalp power above 20 Hz fell **10–200-fold**, meaning most of what the scalp records above 20 Hz is muscle ([Whitham et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17574912)). Our 22–30 Hz band sits on the forehead-muscle peak. With an ear reference, jaw and temple muscle next to the ears can feed into every channel (an inference from Goncharova's maps, not measured). Rewarding low 22–30 Hz is therefore largely muscle-relaxation biofeedback. That may help an over-aroused person, but it isn't brain training. Down-training does not *recruit* muscle the way up-training does (0 of 6 in Paluch's down arm) ([Paluch et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5357644/)). The risk runs the other way: relaxation gets passed off as a brain change.

What `high_beta_down` needs is first an honest description. If cortical high beta is really the goal, it also needs a muscle detector above 30 Hz (for example 45–70 Hz) that **pauses scoring** rather than counting a clench as failure. A local-difference montage at central sites would help further. At 0.4 % reward time [Ours], the current "250 ms of unbroken low" rule almost never fires, because the true 22–30 Hz outline is spiky. A forgiving hold and an easier threshold are prerequisites for it to work at all.

## Alpha up-training: the strongest mechanism data, the weakest outcome data

*Applies to `alpha_up.refrain` (8–12 Hz with a high-beta inhibit).*

Alpha holds the field's best-controlled evidence on *how* feedback works. In two sham- or mock-controlled studies at P4 with eyes open and continuous visual feedback, training raised **how often alpha bursts start, not how big or long they are** ([Ossadtchi et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5476573/); [Belinskaya et al. 2020](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.07.27.222786.full.pdf)) **[Trial, small]**. The second study also showed that this depends on delay: 244 ms total worked, 494 ms worked weakly, and 744 ms did not. The authors suggest rewarding burst *onsets* as a discrete event, which they did not test. With eyes closed, alpha is already near its ceiling. In a 24-person study with continuous pitch feedback, alpha rose only with eyes open ([Biswas & Ray 2019](https://www.eneuro.org/node/1133833.full.print)) **[Trial, within-subject control]**. The controlled studies mostly tailor the band to each person's alpha peak (peak ±2 Hz, after checking the peak stands out from neighbouring frequencies) rather than using fixed 8–12 Hz ([Belinskaya et al. 2020](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.07.27.222786.full.pdf)). Upper-alpha studies report 21–50 % non-learners ([review, arXiv 1903.10425](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.10425)).

For relaxation the outcome evidence is weak. A 50-person double-blind study raised alpha but found **no change in behavioural stress measures** ([Dekker et al. 2012](https://www.scitepress.org/PublishedPapers/2012/38889/38889.pdf)), and another found anxiety and relaxation improved equally with and without real feedback ([PMID 34531416](https://neuinfo.org/literature/pmid:34531416?rpKey=on)) **[Trial]**.

What `alpha_up` needs: an **always-on alpha tone** (every positive alpha study used continuous feedback), the fastest path we can manage, and a ding on burst onset or a short hold rather than 250 ms unbroken plus an AND. It also needs an eyes-open default and an optional individual-peak band. The high-beta inhibit is a reasonable muscle detector, but muscle trainees in Paluch's study raised *alpha* too ([Paluch et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5357644/)). So muscle should mute the whole output, alpha tone included, rather than act as one AND condition among others.

## Alpha-theta: a slow-state protocol being judged on a fast clock

*Applies to `alpha_theta.refrain`, `alpha_theta_narrowband.refrain` and `alpha_theta_narrowband_melodic.refrain`.*

The original Peniston-Kulkosky design used one electrode at O1, eyes closed and **two tones**, one for alpha above threshold and one for theta above threshold. It was preceded by hand-warming training and a guided "sink down" script, and each session ended with a talk about the imagery. The first alcohol study used 15 thirty-minute sessions with 10 people per group ([Peniston & Kulkosky 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2524976/); [Sokhadze et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2259255/)) **[Small/uncontrolled]**. The largest randomized addiction trial (121 inpatients) ran SMR/beta training *first* and alpha-theta after, so the alpha-theta part cannot be isolated ([Scott et al. 2005](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1081/ADA-200056807)) **[Trial]**. The overall rating is **"probably efficacious" as an add-on to inpatient treatment**. Alpha-theta has never beaten sham or meditation ([Sokhadze et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2259255/)). In performance work, real feedback (against mock) raised the theta/alpha ratio within sessions ([Egner, Strawson & Gruzelier 2002](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/5227)), and in musicians that learning tracked performance gains. That setup was Pz, theta 5–8 Hz and alpha 8–11 Hz, headphones, ten 15-minute sessions, learning measured across **3-minute** periods, and artifact thresholds that interrupted feedback ([Egner & Gruzelier 2003](https://web.wpi.edu/Images/CMS/HUA-CIMA/2002_egner.pdf)) **[Trial, small, essentially one lab]**. Our bands (4–8/8–12, 6–8/9–11) and sites (Pz/O1) all fall within the historical range.

The key point for us is timescale. "Crossover", theta rising above a falling alpha, was described in meditators over minutes. The only empirical definition comes from 182 session graphs in 10 case files: imagery reports clustered when theta exceeded alpha by **at least 1 µV for at least 3 minutes** ([Johnson et al. 2013](https://isnr-jnt.org/article/view/16502)) **[Small/uncontrolled]**. No source supports a sub-second or 1-second crossover, and none says fast latency matters for alpha-theta. A Peniston-trained clinician wrote that equipment and filtering differences did not noticeably change outcomes over 18 years ([White 2008](https://isnr-jnt.org/article/view/16667/10630)) **[Convention]**.

That explains why `alpha_theta` fell from 39.9 % to 0.7 % [Ours]. Its reward requires a theta/alpha *ratio* to stay above target for **1 s unbroken**, and a ratio of two flickering outlines flickers even more. **This family is the one place where going back to slow smoothing (500 ms or more) is correct.** Better still, judge crossover on a ratio averaged over several seconds, or count the time spent in crossover over a minute. The continuous ratio tone that `alpha_theta` already plays ungated is the right instinct. Keeping two tones, one per band, would match the original. The delta mute is a sensible stand-in for "relax deeply without falling asleep", and the muscle mute matches the practice of interrupting feedback on artifact. Neither has been tested. The melodic variant's sound design has no evidence either way, because too few sound-only studies exist to judge sound against visual feedback ([Galang et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12426165/)).

## Coherence and asymmetry work on a timescale of seconds

*Applies to `alpha_coherence.refrain` and `faa_f3f4.refrain`.*

**`alpha_coherence`.** No controlled trial of *alpha* coherence training was found. The one well-documented controlled study trained **SMR** coherence between Cz and CPz in 20 healthy adults. Up-training raised coherence and improved memory scores. **Down-training did nothing.** SMR *power* also rose, which muddies what was learned ([Kober et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2020.606684/full)) **[Small/uncontrolled]**. Clinical "coherence normalisation" is uncontrolled. Its own leading practitioners now say the standard two-channel method "is now understood to lead to erroneous findings", because nearby sites and a shared reference make coherence look higher than it is ([Coben et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2018.00729/full)). A review found no empirical support for 19-channel z-score training ([Coben, Hammond & Arns 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6373269/)). Our file computes coherence on 2-s windows with both channels on the amplifier's shared reference. Coherence is a *seconds-scale* number, so the 250 ms hold has no real meaning, and the honest latency is about 1–3 s (inferred from how the estimator works). Kober's design is a usable template: a continuous coherence bar, power shown alongside, a threshold at the baseline average reset after each run (about 50 % reward), and a discrete reward only when everything is in range. Train coherence up only.

**`faa_f3f4`.** People can shift frontal alpha asymmetry within sessions, mostly by raising *right* (F4) alpha ([Allen et al. 2001](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/manipulation-of-frontal-eeg-asymmetry-through-biofeedback-alters-/); [Peeters et al. 2014](https://researchinformation.umcutrecht.nl/en/publications/validation-of-a-neurofeedback-paradigm-manipulating-frontal-eeg-a/); [Mennella et al. 2017](https://www.research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3307297/2/Mennella%20et%20al.pdf)) **[Trial, small]**. Durability is the problem. In a 60-person sham-controlled trial, the change was **gone by one week**, with no mood effect ([Quaedflieg et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4692315/)). Mennella found reduced anxiety but no change in depression, and the clinical rating is "possibly efficacious" ([Neural Plasticity 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7878101/)). The working parameters in these studies are 2-s analysis windows, a log ratio of right to left alpha, smoothing out to about 5 s, a threshold at baseline average plus 0.85 SD (about 20 % reward), **one point per 10 s accumulated in the target state**, and feedback blocked when eye movement exceeds ±50 µV. The choice of reference (Cz against linked mastoids) is disputed. Our file uses a plain F3/F4 alpha ratio with a 500 ms hold. Because thresholds are percentiles, a plain ratio ranks the same as a log ratio, so that part is fine. What it needs is a declared 1–5 s latency, accumulating points instead of a short hold, blink gating, and a check that the reward follows the left/right *balance* rather than total alpha going up.

## Critical fluctuation is an untested idea, and HRV is the best-supported protocol

**`critical_fluctuation.refrain`.** No neurofeedback or brain-computer-interface study has used "early-warning" signals as feedback. Early-warning signals here are rising variance and rising self-similarity moment to moment (autocorrelation) before a system tips into a new state. They appear cleanly in single neurons about to fire ([Meisel et al. 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4338190/)). In long intracranial recordings they track seizure risk over **daily and multi-day cycles** ([Maturana et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7195436)). A large study using shuffled-data controls found **none** in the minutes before 105 seizures ([Wilkat et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08973)). For sleep onset there is only a theory preprint ([arXiv 2608.03000](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.03000)). Our file already does one thing right: it computes the indicators on an *unsmoothed* outline and smooths only the result, as Maturana did. Smoothing beforehand would build in most of the self-similarity regardless of the brain (simple filter arithmetic from the notes). Its 4-second self-similarity window is short, because ecology convention uses windows of 25–75 % of the series ([earlywarnings docs](https://rdrr.io/cran/earlywarnings/man/generic_ews.html)). Note also that switching filters changes the indicator's baseline level. This protocol should be labelled experimental, used as a slow unrewarded cue, and validated against shuffled data before anyone claims that it predicts drowsiness.

**`hrv_resonance.refrain`.** This has the strongest evidence in the library. A 58-study meta-analysis found a small-to-moderate effect, largest for anxiety and depression ([Lehrer et al. 2020](https://scholarlyworks.lvhn.org/select-program/456)), and a 24-study meta-analysis found g ≈ 0.8 for stress and anxiety, though with many small, bias-prone studies ([Goessl et al. 2017](https://open.bu.edu/handle/2144/26911)) **[Trial, meta-analysis]**. The standard protocol finds each person's resonance breathing rate (2-minute trials at 6.5, 6.0, 5.5, 5.0 and 4.5 breaths/min) and then feeds back the **beat-to-beat heart-rate wave with a breathing pacer**. Low-frequency power is a secondary score, and it needs **about 90 s** of data to be valid ([Shaffer & Meehan 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7578229/)). At 0.1 Hz one wave takes 10 s, so filter latency in the EEG sense doesn't apply. Our file band-passes the heartbeat series and smooths over 4 s. It needs the heart-rate wave and pacer as the fast display, power as a slow score, per-person rate calibration, and a beat-plausibility check *before* the filter. A single missed or extra beat creates a large spike that the filter would otherwise reward.

## Latency matters for bursts, and the cFIR vs IIR choice matters little

**Does feedback latency matter?** For burst rhythms, probably yes, but the evidence is a single study. The only EEG experiment that varied delay deliberately (alpha, about 10 people per group, continuous feedback, no threshold) found lasting change in burst rate at 244 ms total, less at 494 ms and **none at 744 ms**. The proposed reason is that late feedback lands after the burst has ended ([Belinskaya et al. 2020](https://publications.hse.ru/mirror/pubs/share/direct/424296808.pdf)) **[Trial, small]**. The often-quoted "under 250–350 ms" rule comes from 1940s rat learning studies, not EEG ([Sherlin et al. 2011](https://isnr-jnt.org/article/view/16552)) **[Expert theory]**. The 2025 update proposes an "optimal window around 300–500 ms" tied to dopamine timing without reconciling it with "as small as milliseconds" ([Kerson et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13525051/)) **[Expert theory]**. For brain-computer interfaces, the sense of control barely changed below 1 s of delay ([Evans et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0130019)). Nobody has measured end-to-end latency for any named commercial system. The "300–1000 ms" figure is a researcher's claim. No study varies latency for SMR or beta. **Where we sit [Ours]:** the old 568 ms envelope plus system delay lands between Belinskaya's weak and null groups. The new 360 ms lands near her middle group. A hold timer adds its own time to the *ding*, but not to an always-on tone. That is the strongest practical argument for the always-on tone: it carries the fast information, while the ding is free to wait for "sustained". For alpha-theta, coherence, asymmetry, HRV and critical fluctuation, nothing suggests sub-second latency matters.

**cFIR (`band_envelope`) or the IIR complex demodulator?** In the main benchmark (resting alpha, 10 people), the cFIR family beat simple rectification, FFT-based Hilbert and forecasting methods at every delay, and the weighted cFIR variant was best at catching bursts ([Smetanin et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1088/1741-2552/ab890f)) **[Trial-grade bench study]**. **No published study compares an IIR complex demodulator with cFIR.** An IIR complex demodulator shifts the band down to zero frequency and then low-pass filters it. Our result (IIR demodulator 312 ms, r 0.933; cFIR 360 ms, r 0.915) [Ours] was scored against a Butterworth-shaped reference. That reference favours the Butterworth-based method, so part of its lead is built into the test. Even taken at face value, a 48 ms and 0.02 correlation difference is small next to the 250 ms steps that showed effects in learning. Whole-system delay (amplifier, Bluetooth, audio buffer) probably matters more than this choice. The sensible path is to re-score both against a neutral "ideal" reference, check how each behaves at burst edges (narrow IIR filters ring), measure true end-to-end delay with a sound or light loop-back as Belinskaya did, and then pick either. Newer approaches (Kalman trackers, neural networks claiming under 10 ms) exist, but they were validated on alpha and are not yet tied to learning outcomes ([Semenkov et al. 2023](https://airi.net/articles/real-time-low-latency-estimation-of-brain-rhythms-with-deep-neural-networks); [Wodeyar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8536256)).

**Continuous or discrete?** No EEG trial pits a graded tone against a binary reward. The argument for graded feedback is that people who fall just short of a binary threshold drop strategies that were working ([Hardt & Kamiya 1976, via Sherlin 2011](https://isnr-jnt.org/article/view/16552)). The argument for discrete reward rests on a mechanism nobody has observed ([Kerson et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13525051/)). In the healthy-adult meta-analysis, 43 of 45 studies used continuous feedback, too few used the alternative to compare, and **audiovisual or game-like feedback beat simple visual bars** during training ([Galang et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12426165/)) **[Trial, meta-analysis]**. Findings from fMRI favouring intermittent feedback come from blood flow's 4–6 s lag and do not carry over to EEG. The proposed hybrid (graded tone, discrete ding, mute on artifact) is consistent with all of this and with the requirement that feedback say "whether the response was right or wrong **and to what extent**" ([Sherlin et al. 2011](https://isnr-jnt.org/article/view/16552)). One caution: an always-on tone tracking SMR or beta will also track muscle that leaks into those bands. The muscle mute must silence the tone as well as the ding, and its threshold must be set per person ([Paluch et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5357644/)). No trial has compared a mute/pause with an inhibit that withholds reward.

## Conclusion

The literature does not ask us to choose between a fast signal and a steady reward. It asks us to stop using signal smoothing to buy steadiness. The old pipeline produced reasonable reward rates by blurring the brain, at the cost of delivering feedback after the bursts it meant to reward had ended. The new filter tells the truth and exposes reward rules that only worked on a blurred signal. The field's own practice already separates the two jobs, with a hold time and lockout in the reward logic and no extra delay in the signal. So the real design work is in reward logic and threshold policy, not in filter choice. The split also falls cleanly by protocol: burst protocols (SMR, beta, alpha) want speed; slow-state protocols (alpha-theta, coherence, asymmetry, HRV) do not, and alpha-theta should be judged over seconds to minutes.

A second takeaway is about where effort pays off. Among standard protocols, band choice did not predict outcome, while signal quality and artifact control did. The best-blinded trials are null at group level, and a third to a half of trainees don't learn. The highest-value additions to the library are therefore probably not new protocols. They are per-person muscle calibration, session logs that show whether a trainee is actually learning the trained band (and not muscle), and measured end-to-end latency for each protocol. Those turn our protocols from plausible into checkable, and nothing in the literature currently provides them.

## Open questions and gaps

These are places where the literature is silent or our own data are not yet enough. They are listed separately so they aren't mistaken for findings.

1. **Forgiving-hold settings.** No study gives the right dip tolerance, two-line gap or hold length for SMR, beta or alpha. We need to replay our 13 recordings with candidate rules and pick settings that land at 25–40 % reward (easier early). After that, real sessions have to show whether people learn.
2. **The hybrid design itself.** No EEG trial compares always-on tone plus ding with a gated tone or a discrete-only reward. Ours would be among the first, which makes logging learning per session essential.
3. **SMR- or beta-specific latency.** No study varies latency for 12–15 Hz or 15–18 Hz, or for threshold-based reward. Everything here is extrapolated from one alpha study with about 10 people per group.
4. **End-to-end latency of our own system.** Our 360 ms figure covers the envelope only. Amplifier, Bluetooth and audio delays are unmeasured. A loop-back measurement per device is needed before declaring latency.
5. **cFIR against IIR on a fair reference.** No published comparison exists. Our comparison favoured IIR by construction.
6. **Auto against fixed thresholds.** The critique of rolling 2-minute percentile thresholds is theory. Nobody has compared the two directly.
7. **Mute against inhibit for muscle.** No trial compares pausing all feedback with withholding reward. Paluch's per-system calibration point means we also need our own muscle thresholds per amplifier.
8. **Ear reference and muscle.** No study quantifies how much an ear reference adds jaw muscle to the 22–30 Hz band. This matters for `high_beta_down` and every high-beta inhibit.
9. **Alpha coherence specifically.** The only controlled coherence trial trained SMR, so alpha coherence rests on extrapolation.
10. **Critical-fluctuation cues.** There is no empirical evidence of early-warning signals before sleep onset, and no neurofeedback use at all.
11. **Sound design for eyes-closed protocols.** There is no evidence on melodic against plain tones, or one tone against two, for alpha-theta.
12. **Sources not retrieved.** Several primary papers were seen only as abstracts or second-hand summaries: Sterman's original papers, Hardt & Kamiya 1976, Peniston's methods text, and the full Kerson 2025 recommendations beyond about 80 % of the text. Details attributed to them should be treated as provisional.
